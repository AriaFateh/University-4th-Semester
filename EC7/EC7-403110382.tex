\section*{Problem 2}

\begin{enumerate}[label=(\alph*)]
	\item \textbf{Slip $s$:} Since the load torque remains constant, the ratio $\frac{s}{R_2}$ must remain constant. Therefore, to compensate for the doubled resistance, the slip $s$ must \textbf{double}.
	\item \textbf{Motor speed $n_m$:} Motor speed is given by $n_m = n_{\text{sync}}(1 - s)$. Because the slip $s$ has increased (doubled), the motor speed $n_m$ will \textbf{decrease}.
	\item \textbf{The induced voltage in the rotor ($E_R$):} The rotor induced voltage is given by $E_R = sE_{R0}$ (where $E_{R0}$ is the locked-rotor voltage). Since the slip $s$ doubles, the induced rotor voltage $E_R$ will \textbf{double}.
	\item \textbf{The rotor current ($I_R$):} At low slip, $I_R \approx \frac{sE_{R0}}{R_2}$. Because both the slip $s$ and the rotor resistance $R_2$ double, the changes cancel each other out. Thus, the rotor current remains \textbf{approximately the same}.
	\item \textbf{Induced Torque ($T_{\text{ind}}$):} The motor must overcome the mechanical load. Since the load is a constant 27\% torque load, the induced torque remains \textbf{unchanged}.
	\item \textbf{Output Power ($P_{\text{out}}$):} Output power is $P_{\text{out}} = T_{\text{load}} \omega_m$. The torque is constant but the mechanical speed $\omega_m$ has decreased, so the output power \textbf{decreases}.
	\item \textbf{Rotor Copper Losses ($P_{\text{RCL}}$):} Rotor copper loss is calculated as $P_{\text{RCL}} = 3 I_R^2 R_2$. Since $I_R$ is constant and $R_2$ is doubled, the rotor copper losses will \textbf{double}.
	\item \textbf{Overall efficiency ($\eta$):} Efficiency is $\eta = \frac{P_{\text{out}}}{P_{\text{in}}}$. Because output power decreases and $I^2R$ internal losses increase while input power remains relatively similar, the overall efficiency \textbf{decreases}.
\end{enumerate}

\section*{Problem 6}
When an induction generator operates alone (isolated from the main power grid), it lacks the external reactive power usually supplied by the grid to excite its magnetic field. Instead, it must be self-excited, typically by a three-phase capacitor bank connected across its stator terminals.

The terminal voltage of a stand-alone induction generator is controlled and determined by four primary factors:

\begin{itemize}
	\item \textbf{The Size of the Capacitor Bank (Primary Control):} 
	The terminal voltage is established at the exact point where the V-I characteristic line of the capacitors intersects the magnetization curve of the induction machine. Increasing the capacitance decreases the capacitive reactance ($X_C$), which lowers the slope of the capacitor line, moving the intersection point higher up the magnetization curve and resulting in a higher terminal voltage.
	
	\item \textbf{The Machine's Magnetization Curve:} 
	The inherent design of the induction machine and the magnetic saturation of its iron core determine the shape of the voltage build-up curve. Without magnetic saturation, the voltage would theoretically increase to infinity; saturation is what stabilizes the voltage at a specific operating point.
	
	\item \textbf{Prime Mover Speed:} 
	The rotational speed of the shaft driven by the prime mover dictates the operating frequency. A higher rotational speed increases the induced voltage for a given flux, effectively shifting the magnetization curve upward and increasing the resulting terminal voltage.
	
	\item \textbf{The Connected Load:} 
	Once a load is connected, it draws active and (often) reactive power. Induction generators have very poor voltage regulation, meaning the terminal voltage will collapse rapidly as the load increases. To maintain a constant terminal voltage under varying loads, the capacitive reactive power supplied to the generator must be continuously adjusted (often through power electronics like a Static VAR Compensator).
\end{itemize}
